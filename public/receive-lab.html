Morbraii — listening experiment: can this browser read Morse out of sound?

An experimental prototype. Not a feature, not finished, and it makes mistakes — see the results so far, below.
What this is

This page tries to hear Morse and turn it back into letters. Your audio is processed here in the browser and never leaves this device — there is no upload, no account, and no external service is called. The only things this page fetches from the internet are its own small summary file and, if you press play, a clip from this same site.

Results so far: loading summary…

Honest limits you should know before you trust anything below: on a clean recording it read SOS correctly at 5 and 12 wpm; at 12 dB of noise one noise burst merged into the first dot and turned SOS into DOS; under reverb it decoded the wrong letters entirely. No human has ever tested this, no phone has ever run it, and nothing here has ever heard a real room, a real hand, or a real buzzer.

2 · What it heard, and what it decided

—

What that confidence figure is — and is not. It is a fit-error readout: how far the measured sound and gap durations sit from the ideal values for the speed the decoder picked. It is not a probability that the decode is right. The highest confidence this test set has ever produced is 60 % — exactly this page's own 60 % bar for asserting a decode — and that 60 % was given to a message that was wrong (a false onset 0.7 s early made SOS read as ESOS). A high number means the numbers fitted neatly, not that the letters are true.

3 · The measurement behind that decode

How the decode works (and what it cannot know)

Envelope. The microphone (or, in the simulation, the recording) is sampled in 256-sample hops (≈5.8 ms at 44.1 kHz) and each hop's RMS becomes a decibel value.

Adaptive threshold. The noise floor is the 20th percentile of the last second of envelope values — measured from the signal, never a fixed number. A sound starts at floor + onset dB and ends at floor + release dB.

Speed, from the signal. Every unit length from 20 ms to 700 ms is tried; each candidate rounds the measured sounds to the nearest of 1 or 3 units and the silences to the nearest of 1, 2, 3, 6, 7 or 14 units (standard and Morbraii spacing are allowed in the same search), and the candidate with the smallest average error wins.

Confidence is a fit-error readout — not a probability of being right — and is how far the measured durations sit from their ideal values, penalised for short messages and for how little the unit length could move before a letter would change. Under 60 % the page calls a decode tentative rather than asserted — and every result in the test set so far came in under 60 %.

What it cannot know. With fewer than two sounds there is no evidence at all for a unit length, so it refuses to decode. It cannot tell a letter gap from an element gap when only one gap is present, and it has no dictionary — a wrong dot/dash decision becomes a real but wrong letter.

No microphone available

Nothing on this page can decode sound without a microphone. So here is what you can still do:

  • (a) Hear the signal. Play a known clip out loud (section 4). You will hear exactly what the decoder is meant to read. If you do have a working microphone, press Listen now again while it plays and the live readout above will try to decode it.
  • (b) Run the decoder on a signal with no microphone at all — section 5, the decoder simulation. That one is not a microphone test, and the page says so on it.

Decoder simulation Not a microphone test

This section feeds a signal straight into the decoder inside this page. No microphone is opened, nothing is played out loud, and no sound has to reach this device. It shows the decoder's own arithmetic on a known recording — it is not evidence that the microphone path works on your device, in your room, with your phone. For that, use Listen now with a real microphone.

No audio hardware is involved at all here: the clip's samples are read out of the file and pushed through the same envelope detector, threshold and speed fitting that the microphone path uses.

A simulation run does not always reproduce the earlier test row exactly. Here the recording is fed in one unbroken stream, while the earlier headless runs went through the browser's capture device and lost a small fraction of frames (capture continuity 0.998–1.0002). On the clean and reverb clips that changes nothing much; on the 12 dB noise clip it was enough to change the answer — the harness read DOS, this simulation reads SOS. Which of the two a real microphone would give you is not known, and running the simulation here therefore does not update the results summary at the top of this page.
